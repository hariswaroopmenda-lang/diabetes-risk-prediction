DIABETES RISK PROJECT
Short project introduction and presentation notes

HOW I WOULD INTRODUCE THE PROJECT
I compared three models on the Pima diabetes dataset. The project ranks a whole
batch of patients because the clinic can only test 30% of walk-ins. I used
validation data to choose a model, then checked its results on a separate test
set. The app can score one example record or rank a batch.

1. Which data did you use?
The CSV has 768 patient records, eight input measurements and an Outcome column.
It is the Pima diabetes dataset. This project uses a public copy hosted by Plotly;
the assignment also links to the dataset on Kaggle. See data/SOURCE.txt.

2. Why did you treat some zeros as missing?
The dataset uses zero to stand in for missing glucose, blood pressure, skin
thickness, insulin and BMI measurements. Zero pregnancies can be real, so I kept
it. I filled missing measurements using medians from the training group only.

3. Why use the median?
The median gives a simple typical value and is less affected by extreme values
than the mean. It is only an approximation, especially for columns with many
missing measurements.

4. Why did you scale some features?
KNN compares distances, so features with larger numeric ranges could otherwise
have more influence. Scaling also helps Logistic Regression. A Decision Tree
splits on one feature at a time, so scaling is not needed for that model.

5. Why split the data into three groups?
The model learns from 460 training rows. I use 154 validation rows to choose a
model and a threshold, then report final results on 154 test rows. Keeping the
test rows separate helps prevent the evaluation from influencing model choice.

6. What does “stratified split” mean?
It keeps roughly the same share of positive and negative outcomes in each group.
That matters here because fewer records have Outcome = 1.

7. What is data leakage?
Data leakage happens when information from the held-out rows changes how a model
is prepared or selected. The imputer and scaler learn their settings from the
training rows. Validation helps with selection; the test results come afterward.

8. Which model did you select, and why?
Logistic Regression had the best validation recall at the 30% capacity, with
precision and average precision used to break ties. I chose it before checking
the final test results. The Decision Tree did better on this particular test
batch. Switching to it afterward would mean using the test set to pick the model.
Results could differ on another split.

9. What was the test result at the 30% capacity?
The selected model chose 46 of 154 test records. Of the 54 positive cases,
it selected 27. Its recall at capacity was 50.0%. That means it caught half of the
positive cases in this test group and missed the other half.

10. What is precision?
Precision is the share of selected patients who were positive in the dataset.
Here it was 27 out of 46, or 58.7%.

11. Why not choose the model with the highest accuracy?
Accuracy counts both positive and negative cases. The clinic's main requirement
is to catch positive cases despite limited testing capacity, so recall at 30%
is a closer match to the stated goal.

12. Why does the app use ranking as well as a score threshold?
The validation threshold for the selected model is 0.468382. The number of
patients above a threshold can change from batch to batch. Ranking the batch and
selecting floor(30% of its rows) always follows the capacity limit. Ten patients
give three slots. A batch under four gives no slots under this strict rule.

13. What does the confusion matrix show?
It counts correct and incorrect decisions. For the selected model at 30% capacity,
there were 27 true positives, 19 false positives, 27 false negatives and
81 true negatives. The report includes the matrices for all three models.

14. Is the app ready for use in a clinic?
No. It is a coursework project, not a diagnostic tool. The data may not represent
the clinic, the model score is not a calibrated medical risk, and the test group
is small. Glucose and insulin also require lab measurements, so these features
do not establish a practical way to screen people before a blood test.

15. What could be improved next?
I would compare settings using cross-validation on the development data, check
how well scores are calibrated, and evaluate a final model on independent data
that represents the clinic's patients. Any clinical use would need expert review.
